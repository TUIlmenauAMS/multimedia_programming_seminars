1. Diffuse (Lambert) Lighting

Lighting in real-time graphics starts with the surface normal — a unit vector perpendicular to the surface at each point. The Lambert (diffuse) model says that the brightness of a surface patch depends only on the angle between the normal N and the direction toward the light L: surfaces facing the light are bright; surfaces facing away receive no light.

The formula is: diffuse = max(dot(N, L), 0). The dot product gives the cosine of the angle (1 when parallel, 0 at 90°, negative when facing away — clamped to 0). An ambient term is added so surfaces facing away from the light are not pure black.

Lambert formula: color = baseColor × (ambient + max(dot(N, L), 0)) — where N is the surface normal and L is the unit direction toward the light.

Flat vs smooth normals: the cube has four identical normals per face (flat shading — each face is one constant colour). The sphere has a unique normal per vertex pointing directly outward from the centre (smooth shading — brightness varies continuously). That is why the cube looks faceted and the sphere looks rounded.

The light is in world space and does not rotate with the model. Move the azimuth/elevation sliders with auto-rotate on to confirm: the lit side of the sphere tracks the light, not the model orientation.

Specular highlights (Blinn-Phong) and physically based rendering (PBR) build on this same diffuse foundation by adding reflected-light terms.

2. Interactive Demo

Enable Show normals to visualise surface normals in two ways simultaneously: colour encoding (X→red, Y→green, Z→blue) painted per-pixel by the fragment shader, and white arrows drawn at a grid of sample points showing the actual direction each normal vector points. An arrow pointing straight at you is foreshortened to a ⊙ symbol — rotate the model and it grows into an arrow. The sphere shows a smooth colour gradient with arrows fanning outward; the cube shows 6 flat colours — one per face — with all arrows on each face pointing the same way.

3. How It Works

This demo extends Demo 4 (Rotating Cube) with normals, a second model, and a lighting calculation in the fragment shader. The key additions:

  1. Normal attribute. Each vertex gets an a_normal vec3. For the cube, all four vertices on a face share the same outward-pointing normal (flat shading). For the sphere, each vertex's normal is normalize(position) — on a unit sphere, the position vector already points outward (smooth shading).
  2. Normal matrix. The vertex shader transforms normals by transpose(inverse(mat3(u_model))). This is the correct transform that keeps normals perpendicular to the surface even if the model has non-uniform scaling. For a rotation-only model it equals the upper-left 3×3 of the model matrix.
  3. World-space light. The light direction uniform u_lightDirection is in world space and does not change when the model rotates — only the model matrix changes. The fragment shader normalises both N (needed because interpolation shortens vectors) and L before the dot product.
  4. Two VAOs, one program. The cube and sphere each have their own VAO recording their vertex data layout. Switching models is just a gl.bindVertexArray call — no re-uploading data.
  5. Light direction from spherical coordinates. Azimuth and elevation angles are converted to a Cartesian unit vector: L = (cos(el)·sin(az), sin(el), cos(el)·cos(az)). This gives intuitive sliders for the light position.

Why normalise in the fragment shader? The vertex shader outputs v_normal as a unit vector, but the GPU linearly interpolates varyings across the triangle. A linear blend of two unit vectors is generally shorter than 1 (e.g., the midpoint of (1,0,0) and (0,1,0) has length ≈ 0.707). Calling normalize(v_normal) in the fragment shader corrects this, giving proper lighting across the surface.
