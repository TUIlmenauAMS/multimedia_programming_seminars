Chapter 05: Google Resonance Audio for Web

When you listen to music through headphones, every sound is locked inside your head — there is no sense of a room, a stage, or a space. The sound is inside you, not around you. Spatial audio changes this: a sound source can be placed at any point in three-dimensional space, and the listener hears it as if it is actually there — coming from the left, from above, from behind a wall, from across a large reverberant hall. The difference, heard through headphones on a well-implemented system, is not subtle.

This chapter builds the complete foundation for spatial audio in the browser — from the physics of how human ears localize sound, through room acoustics and Ambisonic mathematics, to a production-quality integration of Google Resonance Audio with Three.js. Every concept is taught from first principles before the SDK is introduced. By the end, you will understand not just how to call the API, but why every parameter exists and what it encodes about the physical world.

1. Spatial Hearing: The Physics and Psychoacoustics

The human auditory system solves a remarkable inverse problem every moment of your waking life: from two one-dimensional pressure signals — the left and right eardrums — it reconstructs a full three-dimensional acoustic environment. The brain determines not just what is making a sound, but precisely where it is in space. Understanding the cues the auditory system uses is the foundation for building convincing spatial audio.

Interaural Time Difference (ITD)

When a sound source is positioned to the right of the listener, the sound wave reaches the right ear slightly before the left. This delay is the Interaural Time Difference. The geometry is straightforward: the head has a diameter of approximately 17–22 cm, and sound travels at 343 m/s at room temperature. The maximum ITD — for a source directly to the side — is approximately 660 µs.

The formula for ITD as a function of azimuth angle \(\theta\) (measured from directly in front) and head radius \(d\):

\[ \text{ITD}(\theta) = \frac{d \cdot \sin\theta}{c} \]

where \(c\) is the speed of sound (343 m/s) and \(d\) is the interaural distance (approximately 0.215 m). The auditory system can detect ITDs as small as 10 µs — a difference that corresponds to a source displaced by less than 1° from the median plane.

ITD is most effective at low frequencies (below approximately 1500 Hz), where the auditory system can track the phase difference between ears. At higher frequencies, the wavelength becomes short enough that phase tracking breaks down — a different mechanism takes over.

Interaural Level Difference (ILD)

The head is not acoustically transparent. At frequencies where the acoustic wavelength is comparable to or smaller than the head diameter — roughly above 500 Hz — the head casts an acoustic shadow on the far ear. The result is an Interaural Level Difference: the near ear receives more energy than the far ear, and the difference is frequency-dependent.

At 500 Hz, the ILD is negligible (the head is acoustically small relative to the wavelength). At 4 kHz, the ILD can reach 25 dB for a source directly to the side. This is why broadband audio — rich in high-frequency content — produces stronger, more convincing lateral localization than pure low-frequency tones.

ITD vs ILD as complementary cues. The auditory system uses ITD predominantly at low frequencies and ILD predominantly at high frequencies. This duplex theory of sound localization, proposed by Lord Rayleigh in 1907, explains why broadband signals produce the strongest and most unambiguous localization: they carry both cues simultaneously. A pure 440 Hz sine wave has ITD but negligible ILD; a pure 8 kHz tone has ILD but no resolvable ITD. The evacuation alarm in Demo 1 is chosen precisely because it carries energy across the full spectrum, activating both cue systems at once.

Head-Related Transfer Function (HRTF)

ITD and ILD together explain lateral localization — left versus right. But the auditory system also localizes sounds in elevation (above vs below), in depth (near vs far), and critically, it distinguishes sounds in front from sounds behind, even when both have the same ITD and ILD. These additional cues come from the Head-Related Transfer Function.

The HRTF is the frequency response of the acoustic path from a point in space to one eardrum, measured through the full physical structure of the head, torso, and — most importantly — the outer ear (pinna). It is defined separately for each direction in space and for each ear. Formally, it is a pair of direction-dependent filter functions \(H_L(\omega, \theta, \phi)\) and \(H_R(\omega, \theta, \phi)\), where \(\omega\) is angular frequency and \((\theta, \phi)\) are the azimuth and elevation of the source.

The pinna introduces direction-dependent spectral notches above 4 kHz. These notches arise from reflections within the pinna geometry that interfere destructively with the direct path at specific frequencies. The frequency of the notch depends on the angle of arrival. The auditory system learns to interpret these spectral notches as elevation cues — a process that happens during early development and continues throughout life as the brain calibrates to the individual's own pinna geometry.

Measuring the HRTF

The HRTF is measured experimentally: a subject (or an anatomically accurate dummy head with microphones placed in the ear canals) is placed in an anechoic chamber. A loudspeaker is moved through hundreds of positions on a sphere surrounding the subject, and an impulse is played at each position. The resulting impulse response recorded at each ear, for each speaker position, constitutes the HRTF dataset. The industry-standard measurement system is the Neumann KU 100 binaural head. Publicly available databases include CIPIC (45 subjects) and SADIE II (20 subjects, full sphere coverage).

Resonance Audio ships with a single generic HRTF dataset — an average across a population. Generic HRTFs work well for lateral localization (ITD and ILD are largely anatomically consistent), but front-back and elevation cues rely on individual pinna geometry and are weaker with a generic HRTF. This is a known limitation of all generic binaural rendering systems.

Externalization

When binaural audio works correctly, the sound is perceived as existing outside the head — in the room, at a specific location in space. This perceptual quality is called externalization. When it fails, the sound collapses to an in-head position — the sensation that the audio is "inside your skull" rather than "out there." Causes of poor externalization include: a generic HRTF that does not match the listener's own anatomy; the absence of pinna cues (which requires broadband high-frequency content); and the absence of room acoustics (even a small amount of reverberation dramatically improves externalization by providing the acoustic context the brain expects for a real sound in a real space).

Why spatial audio requires headphones. Binaural rendering delivers a precisely calculated signal to each ear. Over loudspeakers, the left loudspeaker's signal reaches both ears (crosstalk), destroying the ITD and ILD cues the renderer computed. Headphone reproduction eliminates crosstalk by design — the left channel reaches only the left ear. This is why every demo in this chapter specifies: best experienced with headphones.

2. Room Acoustics: Sound in Enclosed Spaces

In an anechoic chamber, you hear only the direct sound from the source — the sound that travels in a straight line from source to ear. In every other real environment, sound also bounces off walls, floor, ceiling, and objects. This reflected energy arrives after the direct sound, from multiple directions, and continues to arrive — with diminishing energy — for a time that depends on the room's size and the acoustic absorption of its surfaces. The complete acoustic effect of a room on a sound source is captured by the room impulse response.

The Room Impulse Response

The room impulse response (RIR) \(h(t)\) is the signal recorded at a listener position when an instantaneous impulse is emitted at the source position. It is a complete fingerprint of the room for that source-receiver pair. If you know the RIR, you can predict what any sound \(x(t)\) will sound like in that room by convolution:

\[ y(t) = x(t) * h(t) \]

The RIR has two perceptually distinct regions. The first 0–80 ms contain the early reflections: discrete, identifiable echoes from the nearest walls, floor, and ceiling. These carry spatial information — the brain uses early reflection patterns to estimate the size of the room, the distance of the source, and the direction of the nearest surfaces. The region beyond 80 ms is the late reverberation: a dense, diffuse, exponentially decaying tail that carries the room's acoustic signature without directional content.

Absorption Coefficients — The Physics

When a sound wave strikes a surface, its energy is partitioned into three parts: a fraction is reflected back into the room, a fraction is absorbed (converted to heat by internal friction in the material), and a fraction is transmitted through the surface to the other side. The absorption coefficient \(\alpha\) quantifies the fraction of incident energy that is not reflected back:

\[ \alpha = 1 - \frac{E_{\text{reflected}}}{E_{\text{incident}}} \]

\(\alpha = 0\) means a perfectly reflective surface — a rigid, dense wall that returns all incident energy unchanged. \(\alpha = 1\) means a perfectly absorptive surface — all incident energy is removed from the room, none reflected. An open window is the canonical \(\alpha = 1\) case: sound exits through it and never returns.

The mechanism of absorption differs by material class. Porous absorbers — carpet, curtains, acoustic foam, mineral wool — work by viscous friction: sound waves cause air to oscillate rapidly through the pores, converting kinetic energy into heat. They are most effective at mid and high frequencies (above 500 Hz) where the pore geometry matches the acoustic wavelength. Panel absorbers — plywood panels, drywall, wooden ceilings — are thin, flexible membranes suspended in front of an air cavity. Sound pressure drives the panel into resonance, dissipating energy through the panel's internal damping. They are most effective at low frequencies (below 300 Hz), which is why studios combine porous treatment (high-frequency control) with panel absorbers (low-frequency control). Resonant cavity absorbers (Helmholtz resonators) work at a narrow band determined by cavity geometry; they are common in concert hall design.

Why is 'transparent' named that way if it means maximum absorption? The name refers to the room model, not the material: a wall with \(\alpha = 1\) is "transparent" to the room simulation in the sense that it contributes zero reflections — the room behaves as if that wall were not there acoustically. It is acoustically transparent, not visually transparent. This naming is consistent with how anechoic chambers work: the wedge treatment on all surfaces is chosen precisely to absorb all incident energy, making each wall acoustically invisible.

Frequency Dependence

In reality, \(\alpha\) is a function of frequency. Carpet might have \(\alpha = 0.02\) at 125 Hz but \(\alpha = 0.65\) at 4 kHz. Resonance Audio uses a single broadband coefficient per material — a practical approximation that captures the dominant perceptual effect. The coefficients in the SDK are derived from measured values in the mid-frequency range (500 Hz–2 kHz), where most of the perceptual weight of reverberation lies. This approximation is sufficient for real-time interactive use, but professional acoustic design tools such as ODEON and EASE use frequency-dependent absorption across octave bands.

The Sabin — Unit of Acoustic Absorption

The sabin (symbol: m² sabin in SI, or just "sabin" in the original imperial form) is the unit of acoustic absorption. One square meter of a surface with \(\alpha = 1\) contributes exactly 1 m² sabin of absorption. The total absorption of a room is:

\[ A = \sum_{i} \alpha_i \cdot S_i \quad \text{[m² sabins]} \]

where the sum is over all surfaces, \(S_i\) is the area of surface \(i\) in m², and \(\alpha_i\) is its absorption coefficient. Intuitively: a 10 m² carpet with \(\alpha = 0.35\) contributes 3.5 sabins — the same as 3.5 m² of open window. Adding 3.5 sabins to a room that already has 20 sabins of total absorption is a 17.5% increase, which corresponds to a 17.5% reduction in RT60. The sabin makes absorption additive across surfaces and materials, which is what the Sabine formula requires.

RT60 and the Sabine Formula

The most important single-number descriptor of a room's acoustics is the reverberation time RT60: the time, in seconds, for the reverberant sound energy to decay by 60 dB after the source stops. After a sound source is switched off, the remaining energy in the room consists entirely of reflected waves bouncing between surfaces. Each reflection removes a fraction \(\alpha\) of the energy that strikes each surface. The energy decays exponentially — and the rate of that decay depends on how often waves hit surfaces (determined by room size) and how much is removed at each hit (determined by absorption coefficients).

Wallace Clement Sabine derived the fundamental formula experimentally in 1900 by measuring reverberation times in lecture halls at Harvard and correlating them with volume and the amount of seating (the principal absorber at the time):

\[ \text{RT60} = \frac{0.161 \cdot V}{A} \]

where \(V\) is the room volume in m³ and \(A = \sum_i \alpha_i S_i\) is the total absorption in m² sabins. The constant 0.161 s/m has units and derives from the mean free path of sound in a room: in a rectangular room, the average distance a wave travels between successive reflections is \(\bar{\ell} = 4V / S_{\text{total}}\). The rate of energy decay per second is then \(c / \bar{\ell} \cdot \ln(1/(1-\bar{\alpha}))\), which for small \(\bar{\alpha}\) linearises to give Sabine's formula with the constant \(4\ln(10^3)/c \approx 0.161\) s/m at 20 °C.

The Sabine formula is accurate when the mean absorption coefficient \(\bar{\alpha}\) is below approximately 0.3. For highly absorptive rooms — a recording studio with \(\bar{\alpha} > 0.5\) — Sabine overestimates RT60 because it assumes uniform, diffuse energy distribution, which breaks down when surfaces absorb strongly. The Eyring formula is more accurate in this regime:

\[ \text{RT60} = \frac{0.161 \cdot V}{-S_{\text{total}} \cdot \ln(1 - \bar{\alpha})} \]

where \(\bar{\alpha} = A / S_{\text{total}}\) is the mean absorption coefficient. As \(\bar{\alpha} \to 1\), \(-\ln(1-\bar{\alpha}) \to \infty\), correctly predicting RT60 → 0 (anechoic). Demo 2 uses the Sabine formula, which is appropriate because the interactive range spans from fully reflective to fully absorptive rooms where the formula's behaviour is pedagogically correct even at the extremes.

Absorption Coefficients and Material Presets

Resonance Audio exposes room acoustics through named material presets. Each preset encodes a broadband absorption coefficient used in the image source model and late reverberation computation. The physical interpretation is direct: a wall with \(\alpha = 0.03\) (bare brick) reflects 97% of incident energy at every bounce; a wall with \(\alpha = 0.70\) (acoustic ceiling tiles) reflects only 30%. After ten reflections off a brick wall, the energy is \(0.97^{10} \approx 0.74\) of its original value — 1.3 dB down. After ten reflections off acoustic tiles, it is \(0.30^{10} \approx 6 \times 10^{-6}\) — 52 dB down. This is why switching a single wall from marble to curtain produces an immediately audible change in reverb density.

They are two descriptions of the same physical object.

3. Ambisonics: The Full Spherical Sound Field

Binaural rendering with a fixed HRTF dataset solves the reproduction problem for one listener in one listening configuration. But it encodes the sound field in a form specific to that listener and those HRTFs. Ambisonics takes a different approach: it represents the sound field itself — the full three-dimensional distribution of acoustic energy at a point in space — in a format that is completely independent of the reproduction system. The reproduction (binaural decode for headphones, speaker array decode for a loudspeaker rig) is a separate step applied after encoding.

Spherical Harmonics

Ambisonics encodes the sound field using spherical harmonics — a family of functions defined over the surface of a sphere, orthonormal with respect to the spherical surface integral. They are the spherical analogue of Fourier basis functions over a circle. A real spherical harmonic of degree \(l\) and order \(m\) is written \(Y_l^m(\theta, \phi)\), where \(\theta\) is elevation and \(\phi\) is azimuth. The first few orders are:

\[ Y_0^0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}} \quad \text{(omnidirectional)} \] \[ Y_1^{-1} = \sqrt{\frac{3}{4\pi}} \sin\theta \sin\phi, \quad Y_1^0 = \sqrt{\frac{3}{4\pi}} \cos\theta, \quad Y_1^1 = \sqrt{\frac{3}{4\pi}} \sin\theta \cos\phi \]

An Ambisonic representation of order \(N\) uses \((N+1)^2\) spherical harmonic components. Each component is a separate audio signal whose amplitude at any moment encodes the contribution of the sound field to that harmonic pattern. Any sound field can be approximated to arbitrary accuracy by increasing the order — higher order means more components, finer spatial resolution, and more CPU cost.

Ambisonic Encoding

To encode a point source at direction \((\theta, \phi)\) with signal \(s(t)\) into the Ambisonic bus, each spherical harmonic component \(W_{lm}\) of the B-format signal receives a contribution:

\[ W_{lm}(t) = Y_l^m(\theta, \phi) \cdot s(t) \]

For first-order Ambisonics (the classical B-format), the four components are:

\[ W = \frac{s}{\sqrt{2}}, \quad X = s \cos\phi \cos\theta, \quad Y = s \cos\phi \sin\theta, \quad Z = s \sin\phi \]

These four signals can be stored, transmitted, and decoded entirely independently of how they will be reproduced. This is the key architectural advantage: encode once, decode to any playback system.

Binaural Ambisonic Decoding

Resonance Audio targets headphone reproduction. Its decode step takes the internal Ambisonic bus and produces a binaural stereo output by convolving each B-format channel with a set of pre-computed head-related spherical harmonic filters — a technique called magnitude-least-squares (MagLS) binaural Ambisonic decoding. This is equivalent to computing a direction-dependent HRTF-weighted sum across the full Ambisonic representation of the sound field. The result is a stereo signal where every source encoded on the Ambisonic bus is rendered binaurally according to its position.

Head Rotation in the Ambisonic Domain

One of the principal advantages of the Ambisonic representation is that listener head rotation — the fundamental operation in head-tracked VR audio — is computationally cheap. Rotating the listener's perspective is equivalent to rotating the spherical harmonic coordinate system, which is a matrix multiplication on the coefficient domain (a Wigner-D matrix). This costs \(O((N+1)^4)\) multiplications per frame, far less expensive than re-encoding all sources from scratch. Source positions are fixed in the Ambisonic bus; only the rotation matrix changes when the head moves.

Ambisonics separates the sound field from its reproduction. A single Ambisonic master can be decoded to headphones (binaural, as Resonance Audio does), to a 7.1.4 Dolby Atmos speaker array, to a dome of 50 loudspeakers, or to any other playback geometry. The B-format file is the same in all cases — only the decoder changes. This format-agnostic design is why Ambisonics has become the standard internal representation for immersive audio in VR platforms, YouTube 360, and professional post-production.

Chapter 01 covers the Web Audio API in full. This section is not a repeat — it identifies the specific nodes and patterns that are directly relevant to Resonance Audio integration, and explains the one lifecycle rule that every demo in this chapter depends on.

5. Google Resonance Audio SDK: Architecture

Google Resonance Audio is an open-source (Apache 2.0) spatial audio SDK released by Google in 2017, now maintained by the community on GitHub. It implements binaural rendering via third-order Ambisonics, a physically-based shoebox room acoustics model, and HRTF convolution — all in the browser, using the Web Audio API as its substrate. It is used in Google Cardboard, Daydream VR, YouTube 360, and many third-party applications.

Three-layer architecture

Resonance Audio is organized in three layers that map directly onto the physical model:

  • Source layer. Each ResonanceAudio.Source object represents one spatial sound source. It has a position, orientation, directivity pattern, and distance model. Its .input property is a standard Web Audio AudioNode that accepts audio from any source in the graph.
  • Room layer. The room simulation is configured via setRoomProperties. It defines a rectangular (shoebox) acoustic space by its dimensions and six wall materials. Resonance computes early reflections (using the image source method) and a late reverberation tail from the material absorption coefficients.
  • Listener layer. The ResonanceAudio instance itself is the binaural decoder. It maintains the Ambisonic bus, applies the HRTF-based decode, and exposes the binaural stereo output via its .output AudioNode, which connects to AudioContext.destination.

// If the context was already suspended (e.g. page was hidden), resume it.

6. Integrating Resonance Audio with Three.js

Resonance Audio was designed to integrate with Three.js. The setFromMatrix API accepts a THREE.Matrix4 world matrix directly, meaning the audio coordinate system stays in sync with the visual coordinate system by construction, not by manual management. This section explains the full integration contract and the production patterns that every demo in this chapter uses.

The integration contract

  • Three.js owns the visual world: camera, lights, geometry, materials, scene graph.
  • Resonance Audio owns the acoustic world: sources, listener, room.
  • The Three.js camera's matrixWorld is the single source of truth for listener position and orientation — written to Resonance every animation frame.
  • Three.js scene objects that represent sound sources expose their matrixWorld to Resonance every frame. The source object moves in the scene graph; the audio follows automatically.

Creating a spatial source as a Three.js scene object

// CONCEPT: A spatial source is both a Three.js mesh and a Resonance source.
// They are kept in a single object so the mesh's matrixWorld can be passed
// directly to Resonance each frame — no separate position tracking.
function createSpatialSource(scene, resonanceAudio, audioContext, position) {

  // Visual representation — the pulsing emissive sphere
  const mesh = new THREE.Mesh(
    new THREE.SphereGeometry(0.11, 20, 20),
    new THREE.MeshStandardMaterial({
      color: 0x4fc3f7,
      emissive: new THREE.Color(0x4fc3f7),
      emissiveIntensity: 0.3  // driven by audio RMS each frame
    })
  );
  mesh.position.copy(position);
  scene.add(mesh);

  // Co-located point light — intensity also driven by audio RMS
  // This causes the source to cast dynamic shadows that pulse with the audio.
  const light = new THREE.PointLight(0x4fc3f7, 0, 5);
  light.position.copy(position);
  scene.add(light);

  // Resonance source — one per spatial sound source
  const resonanceSource = resonanceAudio.createSource();

  // AnalyserNode: tap the audio signal to drive visual energy readouts
  const analyser = audioContext.createAnalyser();
  analyser.fftSize = 256;
  const energyBuffer = new Uint8Array(analyser.frequencyBinCount);

  // Connect audio: source → analyser → resonanceSource.input
  // The analyser is a pass-through — it reads energy without modifying the signal.
  analyser.connect(resonanceSource.input);

  return { mesh, light, resonanceSource, analyser, energyBuffer };
}

The animation loop — the integration heartbeat

// CONCEPT: The animation loop is where Three.js and Resonance Audio stay in sync.
// Every frame, in this order:
//   1. Advance any moving objects in the scene graph
//   2. Update camera matrixWorld (Three.js does this automatically if camera is in the scene)
//   3. Write camera matrixWorld to Resonance listener
//   4. Write each source mesh matrixWorld to its Resonance source
//   5. Read audio energy from each AnalyserNode and update visual properties
//   6. Render

function animate() {
  requestAnimationFrame(animate);
  const dt = clock.getDelta();

  // Update camera controls (OrbitControls or PointerLockControls)
  controls.update();

  // CONCEPT: setListenerFromMatrix — the single call that keeps audio in sync with visuals.
  // camera.matrixWorld encodes the camera's full world-space position and orientation.
  // Resonance reads position (translation component) and orientation (rotation component).
  // Three.js keeps matrixWorld up to date automatically for objects in the scene graph.
  camera.updateMatrixWorld();
  resonanceAudio.setListenerFromMatrix(camera.matrixWorld);

  // CONCEPT: setFromMatrix — same principle for each sound source.
  // The source mesh moves in the Three.js scene graph.
  // Its matrixWorld is the final world-space transform after all parent transforms.
  // Passing it to Resonance keeps the acoustic position identical to the visual position.
  for (const src of spatialSources) {
    src.mesh.updateMatrixWorld();
    src.resonanceSource.setFromMatrix(src.mesh.matrixWorld);

    // Drive visual intensity from audio energy
    src.analyser.getByteFrequencyData(src.energyBuffer);
    let sum = 0;
    for (let i = 0; i < src.energyBuffer.length; i++) {
      sum += src.energyBuffer[i] * src.energyBuffer[i];
    }
    const rms = Math.sqrt(sum / src.energyBuffer.length) / 255;
    src.mesh.material.emissiveIntensity = 0.1 + rms * 3.0;
    src.light.intensity = rms * 2.5;
  }

  // Render through the post-processing composer (bloom + output pass)
  composer ? composer.render() : renderer.render(scene, camera);
}

7. Distance Attenuation and Source Directivity

Distance attenuation

In free space, the intensity of a point source falls off with the inverse square of distance. In logarithmic (dB) terms, this means a 6 dB drop for every doubling of distance. At 1 m a source has a certain level; at 2 m it is 6 dB quieter; at 4 m it is 12 dB quieter; at 8 m it is 18 dB quieter. This relationship is immediately perceptible in a well-implemented spatial audio system — and it is one of the most important cues the brain uses to judge source distance.

Resonance Audio controls distance attenuation on each source with three parameters:

// source.setMinDistance(r): the effective radius of the source.
// At distances below r, gain is clamped — the source does not become infinitely loud
// as the listener approaches it. 0.5 m is a reasonable default for a loudspeaker.
resonanceSource.setMinDistance(0.5);

// source.setMaxDistance(r): beyond this distance, attenuation stops.
// Useful for ambient sources that should always be audible at a minimum level.
resonanceSource.setMaxDistance(50);

// source.setRolloffFactor(f): scales the rate of attenuation.
// 1.0 is physically correct (inverse square law).
// Values above 1.0 make attenuation faster — useful for localized effects.
// Values below 1.0 make attenuation slower — useful for ambient sources.
resonanceSource.setRolloffFactor(1.0);

Source directivity

Real-world sound sources do not radiate energy equally in all directions. A violin projects most of its energy forward and to the sides. A guitar amplifier has a cardioid pattern — maximum energy in front, nearly silent behind. A kettledrum radiates primarily upward and downward. Matching the acoustic directivity pattern to the physical reality of the source is part of making a spatial audio scene believable.

Resonance Audio controls source directivity with:

// source.setDirectivityPattern(alpha, sharpness)
//
// alpha controls the pattern shape:
//   0.0 → omnidirectional (equal radiation in all directions)
//   0.5 → cardioid (cosine pattern — maximum forward, zero at 180°)
//   1.0 → figure-eight (bidirectional — maximum front and back, zero at sides)
//
// sharpness controls how narrow the pattern is:
//   1.0 → gentle, wide pattern
//   10.0 → very tight beam

// Omnidirectional source (default — a point source)
resonanceSource.setDirectivityPattern(0.0, 1.0);

// Cardioid source — a loudspeaker or guitar amplifier
resonanceSource.setDirectivityPattern(0.5, 1.0);

// Hypercardioid — tighter forward pattern
resonanceSource.setDirectivityPattern(0.75, 2.0);

// Figure-eight — a bidirectional microphone
resonanceSource.setDirectivityPattern(1.0, 1.0);

Source gain

// source.setGain(value): a linear gain multiplier applied to the source output.
// Muting a source: setGain(0) — cheaper than disconnecting the audio graph,
// since reconnecting is expensive. The synthesis chain continues running (zero cost

Source directivity is frequently overlooked but perceptually significant. Walk behind a guitar amplifier with cardioid directivity set — the level drop is dramatic and immediately recognizable. Walk in front of the same amplifier — the level is highest directly in front. This is exactly what you experience in a recording studio. Omitting directivity makes every source sound like a floating point in space with no physical shape; applying it makes the source feel like a real object.

8. Room Acoustics: How the Simulation Works

The shoebox model

Resonance Audio simulates a rectangular (shoebox) room. The room is defined entirely by three numbers — width, height, depth in meters — and six absorption coefficients, one per wall. Real rooms are rarely rectangular, but the shoebox approximation is physically well-motivated and computationally tractable for real-time use. Its accuracy is high for the dominant perceptual effect — the reverb tail shape and density — and acceptable for early reflection timing in moderately sized rooms. Where it fails is in rooms with complex geometry (curved walls, galleries, irregular floor plans), where more expensive methods such as finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) or geometric ray tracing are required.

The room is centred at the origin in Resonance's coordinate system. The six walls are labelled by their axis and direction:

leftX−X wallNegative X face
rightX+X wallPositive X face

backZ−Z wallNegative Z face (where the source is placed)
downY−Y wallFloor

The image source method — early reflections

Early reflections (the first 80 ms after the direct sound) are computed using the image source method, a geometrically exact technique for rectangular rooms. The principle is elegant: a reflection off a flat wall is acoustically identical to a direct signal arriving from a virtual source placed at the mirror image of the real source across that wall. For a rectangular room, this construction can be applied recursively: the mirror image of a mirror image across two walls gives a second-order reflection, and so on.

Concretely: if the real source is at position \(\mathbf{s} = (s_x, s_y, s_z)\) and the room spans \([-W/2, W/2] \times [0, H] \times [-D/2, D/2]\), then the first-order image sources across the left and right walls are:

\[ \mathbf{s}^{\text{left}} = (-W - s_x,\; s_y,\; s_z), \qquad \mathbf{s}^{\text{right}} = (W - s_x,\; s_y,\; s_z) \]

and similarly for the remaining four walls. Second-order images are mirrors of first-order images across adjacent walls, producing 26 second-order virtual sources for a rectangular room. Each image source \(\mathbf{s}^{(k)}\) contributes an early reflection to the listener at \(\mathbf{l}\) with:

  • Delay: \(\tau_k = |\mathbf{s}^{(k)} - \mathbf{l}| / c\) — the travel time from the image source to the listener, determined by distance.
  • Level: attenuated by \(1/|\mathbf{s}^{(k)} - \mathbf{l}|\) (inverse distance law) and by the product of reflection coefficients \((1 - \alpha_i)\) of every wall the reflection crossed.
  • Direction: the direction from the listener toward each image source, used to encode the reflection into the Ambisonic bus at the correct spatial position.

The result is a set of discrete echoes — early reflections — each arriving from a specific direction, at a specific time, with a specific level. These carry the perceptual cues of room size and geometry. The brain is highly sensitive to early reflections: a single lateral reflection arriving 10–30 ms after the direct sound produces a strong sense of "spaciousness" and "envelopment." Resonance computes image sources up to a configurable reflection order (typically first and second order for real-time use).

Late reverberation — the statistical tail

Beyond approximately 80 ms, the reflected energy has bounced many times and arrives from all directions simultaneously. It is no longer possible (or perceptually meaningful) to track individual reflections. This late reverberation region is modelled statistically. Resonance Audio uses a feedback delay network (FDN) — a set of recirculating delay lines with cross-coupling, tuned so the energy density and decay rate match the Sabine prediction for the configured room dimensions and absorption coefficients. The FDN is initialized with parameters derived from the room geometry at the time setRoomProperties is called.

The combined signal path for room simulation in Resonance Audio is therefore:

// Internal Resonance Audio room simulation signal path (conceptual):
//
// Source audio signal s(t)
//        │
//        ├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────▶ Direct path
//        │                                                          (no delay, no attenuation by walls)
//        │
//        ├──▶ Image source method (order 1 and 2)
//        │      For each virtual image source k:
//        │        delay  = distance(image_k, listener) / c
//        │        level  = Π(1 − αᵢ) / distance(image_k, listener)
//        │        direction = normalize(image_k − listener)
//        │      → Encoded into Ambisonic bus at direction, with delay and level
//        │                                                       ▲ Early reflections
//        │                                                         (0–80 ms, directional)
//        │
//        └──▶ Feedback Delay Network (FDN)
//               Parameters derived from: room volume, total absorption A
//               Decay time tuned to match RT60 = 0.161 × V / A
//               → Encoded into Ambisonic bus as diffuse (omnidirectional) energy
//                                                        ▲ Late reverberation
//                                                          (80 ms – RT60, diffuse)
//
// All three paths are summed in the Ambisonic bus → binaural decode → stereo output

What you hear when you change wall materials

When you switch a wall from 'marble' (\(\alpha = 0.01\)) to 'curtain_heavy' (\(\alpha = 0.60\)) in Demo 2, three things change simultaneously in the simulation:

  1. Image source levels drop. The reflection coefficient of that wall changes from \(1 - 0.01 = 0.99\) to \(1 - 0.60 = 0.40\). Every early reflection that bounced off that wall is now 60% quieter. Reflections that bounced off that wall twice are \(0.40^2 = 16\%\) of their original level. This immediately thins the early reflection density.
  2. Total absorption increases. More energy is removed per reflection cycle. The FDN decay time shortens in proportion to the Sabine formula — if the total absorption \(A\) doubles, RT60 halves.
  3. The spatial distribution of late reverberation changes. A heavily absorbing wall acts as a directional sink — reflections from that direction are weaker, so the reverberant field loses energy faster from that angular sector. The late tail becomes asymmetric in the Ambisonic domain.

function computeRT60(dimensions, materials) {
  const { width: W, height: H, depth: D } = dimensions;
  const volume = W * H * D;  // m³

  // Surface areas of the six walls [m²]
  const surfaces = {
    left:  H * D,  right: H * D,   // side walls: height × depth
    front: W * H,  back:  W * H,   // end walls:  width × height
    down:  W * D,  up:    W * D,   // floor/ceiling: width × depth
  };

  // Total absorption A = Σ αᵢ × Sᵢ  [m² sabins]
  let A = 0;
  for (const [wall, area] of Object.entries(surfaces)) {
    const alpha = ABSORPTION[materials[wall]] ?? 0.05;
    A += alpha * area;
  }

  if (A < 0.001) return Infinity;  // fully reflective room — reverb never decays

  // Sabine formula: RT60 = 0.161 × V / A
  return 0.161 * volume / A;  // seconds
}

9. Ambisonics in Resonance Audio

Resonance Audio encodes all sources into an internal Ambisonic bus before the binaural decode step. Understanding how the Ambisonic bus works inside the SDK opens up two advanced capabilities: accessing the raw Ambisonic signal for visualization and analysis, and understanding why head rotation is cheap.

The internal signal path

When resonanceAudio.createSource() is called, Resonance creates an Ambisonic encoder for that source. Each frame, the source's current position (from setFromMatrix) is used to compute the spherical harmonic gains \(Y_l^m(\theta, \phi)\) for the source direction, and the source audio is multiplied by these gains and summed into the shared Ambisonic bus. The room simulation — early reflections and late reverberation — is applied in the Ambisonic domain. Finally, the binaural decoder reads the Ambisonic bus and produces the stereo output.

Setting Ambisonic order

// ambisonicOrder is set at construction time and cannot be changed afterward.
// Order 3 (16 channels) is the recommended default.
//
// If CPU budget is tight (mobile, low-end hardware), order 1 (4 channels) is usable
// but audibly blurry — sources within ~90° of each other may be hard to separate.
// Order 2 (9 channels) is a reasonable middle ground.

const resonanceAudio = new ResonanceAudio(audioContext, {
  ambisonicOrder: 3,  // 16 channels, ~30° spatial resolution
});

Accessing the Ambisonic bus output

// resonanceAudio.ambisonicOutput is an AudioNode with (N+1)² channels.
// At order 3, that is 16 channels — one per spherical harmonic component.
// Use ChannelSplitterNode to route individual channels to separate AnalyserNodes.

// CONCEPT: ChannelSplitterNode extracts individual channels from a multichannel node.
// Channel 0 is W (zeroth-order omnidirectional component).
// Channels 1–3 are X, Y, Z (first-order components — figure-eight patterns).
// Channels 4–8 are second-order, etc.
const splitter = audioContext.createChannelSplitter(16);
resonanceAudio.ambisonicOutput.connect(splitter);

// Connect the first four channels (B-format: W, X, Y, Z) to separate analysers
const bFormatAnalysers = ['W','X','Y','Z'].map((_, i) => {
  const analyser = audioContext.createAnalyser();
  analyser.fftSize = 256;
  splitter.connect(analyser, i);  // channel i → analyser
  return analyser;
});

// In the animation loop: read RMS from each analyser to visualize B-format energy
bFormatAnalysers.forEach((analyser, i) => {
  const buf = new Uint8Array(analyser.frequencyBinCount);
  analyser.getByteFrequencyData(buf);
  let sum = 0;
  buf.forEach(v => sum += v * v);
  const rms = Math.sqrt(sum / buf.length) / 255;
  // rms[0] = W energy (omnidirectional pressure)
  // rms[1] = X energy (front-back figure-eight)
  // rms[2] = Y energy (left-right figure-eight)
  // rms[3] = Z energy (up-down figure-eight)
});

Head rotation — why it is cheap

When the listener rotates their head, Resonance does not re-encode the sources. Source positions are fixed in the Ambisonic bus. Instead, a rotation matrix (a Wigner-D matrix for the current order) is applied to the Ambisonic coefficient domain. For order 3, this is a 16×16 matrix multiplication per audio frame — far cheaper than re-computing spherical harmonic gains for every source from scratch. This architecture is why head-tracked VR audio is feasible at high Ambisonic orders: sources are encoded once per frame update; head rotation costs only the Wigner-D matrix multiply.

// CONCEPT: Head rotation in the Resonance / Three.js integration.
// The camera's quaternion represents the listener's head orientation.
// setListenerFromMatrix reads the rotation component of camera.matrixWorld
// and applies the corresponding Wigner-D rotation in the Ambisonic domain.
// No special call is needed — it is handled automatically by setListenerFromMatrix.

// Head-tracked rotation (e.g. from mouse drag, IMU sensor, or WebXR):
camera.quaternion.setFromEuler(new THREE.Euler(pitch, yaw, 0, 'YXZ'));
camera.updateMatrixWorld();
resonanceAudio.setListenerFromMatrix(camera.matrixWorld);
// ↑ This one call: updates listener position AND applies Wigner-D rotation.
// The Ambisonic bus is not re-encoded — only the decode matrix rotates.

10. Performance and Production Considerations

CPU budget

The real-time cost of Resonance Audio scales with the number of sources on the full Ambisonic bus and the Ambisonic order. At order 3 on a 2022-era laptop, each active Ambisonic source costs approximately 2–4% CPU on the audio thread. Keep active Ambisonic source count below 16 for smooth 60 fps. For scenes with many simultaneous sources, use distance-based LOD: sources beyond a threshold distance contribute negligible spatial detail — the HRTF difference between 20 m and 25 m is below perceptual threshold. Downgrade distant sources to a simple StereoPannerNode and restore them to the Ambisonic bus when they come within range.

// CONCEPT: Spatial audio Level of Detail (LOD).
// Sources far from the listener are downgraded to a cheap stereo pan.
// Sources near the listener use the full Ambisonic rendering path.
// A crossfade at the LOD boundary avoids an audible discontinuity.

const LOD_THRESHOLD = 15; // meters

function updateSourceLOD(source, listenerPosition) {
  const distance = source.mesh.position.distanceTo(listenerPosition);

  if (distance > LOD_THRESHOLD) {
    // Downgrade to stereo — mute the Ambisonic path, enable the stereo path
    source.resonanceSource.setGain(0);
    const pan = computeStereoPan(source.mesh.position, listenerPosition);
    source.stereoPanner.pan.setTargetAtTime(pan, audioContext.currentTime, 0.05);
    source.stereoGain.gain.setTargetAtTime(
      computeDistanceGain(distance), audioContext.currentTime, 0.05
    );
  } else {
    // Restore full Ambisonic rendering
    source.resonanceSource.setGain(source.nominalGain);
    source.stereoGain.gain.setTargetAtTime(0, audioContext.currentTime, 0.05);
  }
}

Occlusion simulation

When a wall stands between a sound source and the listener, two things happen physically: the total energy reaching the listener is reduced (the wall absorbs energy), and the high-frequency content is attenuated more than low-frequency content (the mass law — wall transmission loss is proportional to frequency × surface mass). A first-order approximation simulates this with a low-pass filter and a gain reduction on the occluded source.

// CONCEPT: Occlusion via Three.js Raycaster.
// A ray from the source position to the listener position, intersecting wall geometry,
// detects acoustic occlusion. This uses the same geometry the student sees —
// if a wall is visually between source and listener, the ray hits it.
const raycaster = new THREE.Raycaster();

function checkOcclusion(sourcePosition, listenerPosition, wallObjects) {
  const direction = listenerPosition.clone().sub(sourcePosition).normalize();
  const distance  = listenerPosition.distanceTo(sourcePosition);

  raycaster.set(sourcePosition, direction);
  raycaster.far = distance;

  const hits = raycaster.intersectObjects(wallObjects);
  return hits.length > 0;
}

// Apply occlusion to the audio graph:
function applyOcclusion(source, occluded) {
  // Low-pass filter: walls block high frequencies (mass law).
  // 500 Hz cutoff simulates a standard interior partition.
  source.occlusionFilter.frequency.setTargetAtTime(
    occluded ? 500 : 20000, audioContext.currentTime, 0.05
  );
  // Gain reduction: walls absorb energy (approximately -10 dB for a standard wall).
  source.occlusionGain.gain.setTargetAtTime(
    occluded ? 0.3 : 1.0, audioContext.currentTime, 0.05
  );
}

AudioContext lifecycle

// CONCEPT: AudioContext lifecycle management for long-running scenes.

// The browser suspends AudioContext when the page is hidden (user switches tabs).
// Always resume on visibility restore:
document.addEventListener('visibilitychange', async () => {
  if (document.visibilityState === 'visible' && audioContext) {
    if (audioContext.state === 'suspended') await audioContext.resume();
  }
});

// Never create more than one AudioContext per page.
// Multiple contexts waste CPU and can cause timing drift between audio streams.
// If you need to reinitialize audio (e.g. user clicks Stop then Start),
// close the existing context first:
async function reinitializeAudio() {
  if (audioContext) {
    await audioContext.close();
    audioContext = null;
  }
  audioContext = new AudioContext();
  await audioContext.resume();
  // ... reinitialize Resonance, sources, etc.
}

Synthesis vs file playback

Web Audio synthesis — OscillatorNode, procedural noise via ScriptProcessorNode or AudioWorkletNode, additive synthesis — is always available, requires no network, and cannot have CORS issues. It is the preferred source for demos and prototypes. For file playback, the pattern is: fetch() the audio file (or use a FileReader for local files), call audioContext.decodeAudioData() to decode it into a reusable AudioBuffer, then create a new AudioBufferSourceNode for each playback. AudioBufferSourceNode is single-use — once it has played or been stopped, it cannot be restarted. Create a new one; the buffer is reusable at zero additional cost.

The Web Audio API runs on a dedicated audio thread. Resonance Audio executes on this thread, separate from the main JavaScript thread. Audio processing continues smoothly even when the Three.js render loop stutters under load. The calls that cross threads — setListenerFromMatrix, setFromMatrix, setRoomProperties — write to Resonance's internal state; the audio thread reads this state asynchronously. These calls are cheap from the main thread. Do not throttle them — call them every animation frame.
