Demo 1: Binaural Lab — Pure HRTF

An anechoic chamber rendered in Three.js. No room acoustics, no reverb — the walls absorb everything. A single spatial audio source orbits the listener on a choreographed 3D path: equatorial orbit, elevation sweep, figure-8, or free-roaming. You experience pure HRTF — the interaural time difference (ITD), interaural level difference (ILD), and pinna spectral cues that the brain uses to localize sound in three dimensions.

The HRTF sphere visualization maps the analytical gain pattern at every direction: vertex colors show where the HRTF is loud (warm) and where it is quiet (cool) as the source moves. The toggle switches between Resonance binaural (full HRTF) and a flat StereoPannerNode — the perceptual difference, especially for elevation, is immediately audible.

CONCEPT: Head-Related Transfer Function (HRTF). The HRTF encodes how sound from every direction in space is filtered by the listener's head, pinnae (outer ears), and torso before reaching the eardrums. It is a direction-dependent filter: the same source sounds different from above versus below, front versus behind. The Resonance Audio SDK applies a measured HRTF database via convolution, producing convincing 3D audio over headphones. The key perceptual cues are ITD (interaural time difference — microseconds of delay between left and right), ILD (interaural level difference — dB difference in amplitude), and pinna spectral cues (notches above 4 kHz that encode elevation).
CONCEPT: Why broadband audio for HRTF demonstration. The HRTF is frequency-dependent. Pinna cues operate above 4 kHz — they are the spectral notches and peaks that encode elevation. A pure tone at 440 Hz has no energy above 4 kHz and therefore carries no elevation information. The sawtooth oscillator used here produces harmonics up to ~8 kHz (above that, the oscillator's digital aliasing rolls off), making elevation cues clearly audible. This is why music — which is broadband — localizes better than a pure test tone.
CONCEPT: Anechoic approximation via Resonance room materials. Resonance Audio's room model simulates early reflections and late reverberation via its setRoomProperties() API. Setting all six wall materials to fiber_glass_insulation gives the highest absorption coefficient (~0.99) in the preset library. This minimizes reflected energy and isolates the HRTF cues — no reverb masking. A true anechoic chamber is defined as having less than 10 dB of reflected energy relative to the direct sound; this simulation is an effective approximation for perceptual demonstration purposes.
CONCEPT: HRTF vs StereoPannerNode. The StereoPannerNode is a simple equal-power pan law: it adjusts the amplitude ratio between left and right channels as a function of azimuth only. It has no elevation encoding, no ITD, no pinna spectral shaping. Toggle between modes and notice: in Stereo Pan mode, elevation changes are inaudible — the source sounds identical at 0° and 90° elevation. In HRTF mode, elevation is immediately perceptible, especially with headphones. The 3-second crossfade between modes uses a GainNode ramp to avoid click artifacts (a waveform discontinuity that would cause a loud audible pop).
CONCEPT: HRTF sphere visualization. The colored sphere surrounding the listener head maps the analytical HRTF gain at every direction in space. For each vertex of a 64×32 sphere geometry, the dominant ILD contribution is approximated as: ILD(θ) ≈ 6.0 · sin(θ) where θ is the azimuth in radians (from the Woodworth-Schlosberg spherical head model, simplified). This is not the full HRTF database (that is a measured impulse response — not analytically available in the browser), but it is an educationally accurate visualization of the dominant directional effect. Colors follow a viridis-inspired ramp: blue (low gain) → green → yellow → red (high gain). The sphere updates every frame as the source moves.
CONCEPT: setFromMatrix() is the production pattern. Resonance Audio provides source.setPosition(x, y, z) for simple cases, but the production-correct method is source.setFromMatrix(mesh.matrixWorld). This reads the final world transform from the Three.js scene graph, correctly handling all parent transforms, rotation hierarchies, and scale. If you maintain a separate position variable and call setPosition(), it desynchronizes from the visual whenever the object is in a transform hierarchy. The matrix approach is always correct and always in sync.
